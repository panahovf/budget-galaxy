What does the budget say?

Three views of Canada's federal budgets since 2022: the Map shows how programs connect, Gravity what each budget is made of, and Priorities which themes are rising up the list or falling off it.

 

 

Source: Department of Finance Canada, Budgets 2021 to 2026.

How the numbers are made

What is here. Every measure in Canada's federal Budgets 2022 to 2026, 999 in all: each item a budget chapter announces under its own heading, with the text that describes it, plus the lines of the cost tables that have no heading of their own. Budget 2021 is used in one place only: the Priorities averages for 2022 and 2023.

The money. Each measure's net cost over the five or six years its budget's cost tables cover, matched line by line; every table adds up to the total the budget publishes. A measure that costs money is new spending. One that saves or raises money (a spending review, a tax change) is counted apart, as what pays for it. About one measure in four has no line in the tables: it is drawn small and counts for its words only.

The themes. Each measure was read in full and given one of 15 themes, by what it is for rather than how it is delivered: a tax cut that lowers families' costs is Affordability, a write-off for business investment is Trade, industry & competitiveness. Measures aimed only at Indigenous peoples go to Indigenous. Taxation and Government hold only the tax system and the running of government. Other, mostly international aid (13 measures), is grey and sits out of Priorities.

The Map. Each measure is placed by its words. Words and short phrases are scored by how particular they are to the measure, folded into 100 word patterns, compared measure by measure, and laid out flat (t-SNE) so that measures about similar things sit together. Only closeness counts: the map's directions mean nothing. Shading marks where each theme's measures are densest.

Gravity. A budget's new spending falls into one mass. Each theme is pulled toward the centre by its share of the money: half or more of it puts a theme at the very centre, none leaves it at the rim. Themes with less than 5% of the money are grey. Dot area follows the money, on one scale for every budget, so a bigger budget makes a bigger mass. A budget is focused when one theme takes 35% or more of its new spending, leaning at 25 to 35%, broad below that. Under the mass sits what pays for it: the measures that save or raise money. Budget 2026's is small because the savings from its spending review were already counted in Budget 2025.

Priorities. Money, across: a theme's share of new spending, averaged over the last three budgets, each budget counting equally. Rhetoric, up: its share of the budget's words. Both lines sit at 7%, an even share among the 14 themes. Bubble size: what this budget puts into the theme. Savings are left out of this view.

Same numbers every time. Five Python notebooks read the budgets, match the costs, apply the themes, draw the map and add up the charts, saving their data and checks along the way; running them again gives the same results. The notebooks, data and hand decisions are on GitHub.

Source: Department of Finance Canada, Budgets 2021 to 2026 (budget.canada.ca).
